Renewal & obsolescence · Primer

Renewal risk

At expiry, and at each option date of a long data-centre lease, the tenant decides whether the facility still earns its return. This page sets out what wears that return down, and where this dashboard measures it.

The decision at each option date

A large tenant typically signs for about 15 years, with options to extend: three further terms of five years, say. At each option date it extends if the space still earns a return on the work it runs there, and hands it back if not. The facility's income after the first term rests on those decisions; re-leasing takes time and, if the building no longer suits current hardware, capital.

Whether the tenant can pay is Tenant credit; a tenant able to pay may still choose not to stay. Over a term that spans several hardware generations and several turns in model pricing, two forces wear the return down: the economics of the AI work the tenant runs, and the building itself. The tenant's stated intent for Australia would show a change before either reaches leasing.

The tenant's AI economics

AI workloads in Australian data centres are inference and cloud rather than training: serving models and running cloud services, not building the models. So what bears on a renewal is inference-side: what a token of a given capability costs, and how many get used.

  • Price at constant capability. The price of a fixed level of capability falls as newer models match older ones for less. A tenant selling AI services sees its revenue per token fall at that rate; its return on leased space holds only if volume grows faster. R3 tracks that price at two capability bars, on Model economics.
  • Open weights. When open-weights models reach the same capability for less, the model layer commoditises. That carries two signs: cheaper tokens get used, which moves inference volume and the space it needs (this row); and frontier-lab margins come under pressure, which Frontier labs reads as a question of tenant credit. R1 measures open-weights models' share of the cost–capability frontier; R2, their share of the tokens routed through OpenRouter.
  • Token volume. Inference demand tracks how much AI work is run. Its token evidence, OpenRouter traffic, is developer and aggregator routing rather than enterprise cloud volume: developer mindshare, not market share. Frontier-lab revenue is the paid-adoption side.
  • Payback. A hyperscaler keeps leasing and renewing while its AI capital spending pays back. Its disclosed proxies for payback are capex against cloud revenue, contracted backlog and stated AI run-rates. R4 sets these side by side on Hyperscaler AI ROI; AI & DC CAPEX and Historical Guidance Revisions show the capex and its guidance.

The shell

A building is designed for decades; accelerators turn over every year or two. Successive generations have drawn more power per rack and moved cooling from air towards liquid, raising the power each data hall must deliver. An option in 2040 is exercised only if the shell can house the hardware of 2040: its rack power density, its liquid cooling, its power per hall.

Obsolescence is measured against a stated tier-1 design envelope, the rack power and cooling that a tier-1 operator (the AirTrunk and CDC class) discloses for what it builds today, and never against any actual facility. R5 sets the hardware trajectory against that envelope on Hardware & facility.

For background, Technology Roadmaps carries the Risk Calendar: hardware transitions year by year, and the three clocks (silicon, facility, energy) whose mismatch is this question. System Integration compares cooling by rack density; AI Accelerator Architecture tracks power per chip, and GPU Hardware & Pricing performance per watt.

Australian commitment

The anchor tenants' stated intent for Australia: capital announced, sites identified, and the megawatts in the project database linked to a hyperscaler. A tenant still committing capital and sites in Australia expects to need the capacity; a slowdown in announcements and site leads would show before a slowdown in leasing, and so before a renewal is in doubt. Announcements are statements of intent, not contracted leases. R6 measures the capital announced, on Australian commitment; Project Analysis lists the announcements and the site leads.

Where this row measures it

Each node's dossier holds its signals, its evidence pages and the news it files.

  • Model economics R1 · R3 Price per million tokens at constant capability; open-weights models' share of the cost–capability frontier.
  • Inference demand R2 Token volume and adoption; the open-weights share of OpenRouter tokens.
  • Hyperscaler AI ROI R4 Capex against cloud revenue, backlog and stated AI run-rates; capex guidance from first issue to actual.
  • Hardware & facility R5 Rack power and cooling by accelerator generation, against a stated tier-1 design envelope.
  • Australian commitment R6 Hyperscaler capital announced for Australia, site leads by evidence tier, hyperscaler-linked megawatts.

What this dashboard does not do

It reports numbers, trends and dates: for each signal, what changed, by how much and since when, then its level and age, as the cockpit lists them. It does not grade them: no score, no colour scale, no forecast of whether any tenant will renew. Reading a signal against a particular lease or building is left to the reader.

Market-level observations about participants in the Australian data-centre market, built from public data. Nothing here describes any lender's exposures or counterparties.
