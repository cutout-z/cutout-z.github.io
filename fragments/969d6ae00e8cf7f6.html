Why Your Fancy New Computer Might Lose to an Old RTX 3090

Author: Lotto (@LottoLabs) Published: 7:27 AM · Apr 24, 2026 Engagement: 8 replies · 13 reposts · 117 likes · 110 bookmarks · 40.5K views

If you're shopping for an inference rig to run AI models at home, you've probably stared at specs until your eyes glazed over.

Intel B70! Apple Mac Pro M5 Max! GB10! Ryzen Max ai+!

PCIe 4.0 x16! PCIe 5 x8/x8! GDDR6X! 24GB VRAM! Unified memory! Memory bandwidth! Blackwell!

It's tempting to think that newer equals faster, and that the big "PCIe" number on the box is what determines how fast you'll get a response from your local LLM.

The mix-up everyone makes

You see "PCIe 5.0 x16" and assume that's the speed limit for generating text? Nope. That number describes the highway between your CPU and GPU — not the GPU's internal processing speed.

PCIe: The moving truck

Think of PCIe as the moving truck that hauls your model from system RAM (or your SSD) into the GPU's local memory (VRAM). A faster PCIe lane means a faster load time. But once the model is loaded into VRAM, PCIe is basically done. It's just sitting there waiting for the next load job.

VRAM bandwidth: The conveyor belt inside the factory

VRAM bandwidth is the speed at which your GPU reads and writes its own local memory. Every single token your LLM generates requires reading huge chunks of the model's weights from VRAM. The faster that read happens, the faster you get your next word.

This is why memory bandwidth is the hidden stat that actually matters for local LLM speed.

Why people still hunt for 3090s

Here's the part that surprises people: an older RTX 3090 often gets recommended over newer, "faster" cards. Why? Because the 3090 has 24GB of VRAM and 936 GB/s of memory bandwidth.

So when a model fits entirely in that 24GB of VRAM, the 3090 absolutely cooks. Even if it's "old" and uses PCIe 4.0 instead of PCIe 5.0.

Newer GPUs might look better on paper for gaming or rendering, but if they cheaped out on memory bandwidth or only have 12GB of VRAM, they'll choke on larger models.

That's why you'll see me and others still chasing 3090s instead of some newer cards that technically benchmark higher on synthetic tests.

The pain really hits when your hot working set doesn't fit in VRAM and has to spill over to system RAM — then PCIe speed becomes a painful bottleneck again. But that's a problem you solve by having enough VRAM, not by buying faster PCIe.

The simple rule

Use PCIe bandwidth to think about transfer and offload limits: How fast can I get the model onto the GPU? How bad is the penalty if it spills to RAM?

Use VRAM bandwidth to think about local generation speed: How fast can the GPU feed itself while generating tokens?

In plain English: PCIe is how fast the GPU gets data from the computer. VRAM bandwidth is how fast the GPU thinks.

Don't let marketing distract you from the memory specs that actually determine whether your local LLM setup flies or crawls.
