I Did the Math on OpenAI's Industrial Policy (None of it Adds Up)

Author: Emad (@EMostaque) — Open Sovereign AI @ii_posts. Founder @StabilityAI. Published: 1:03 AM · Apr 9, 2026 Views: 93K | Replies: 23 | Reposts: 52 | Likes: 203 | Bookmarks: 273

OpenAI's April 2026 paper, "Industrial Policy for the Intelligence Age," asks the right question. What happens to people when machines can think? The problems it identifies are real. The displacement is real. The urgency is real.

I agree. Two years ago I moved on from building frontier AI models at Stability AI and founded Intelligent Internet to focus on the answer. The Last Economy laid out the framework. But any plan for this transition needs math that works.

For a company that prides itself on technical rigour, the paper is strikingly devoid of any. No cost projections. No displacement timelines. No tax modelling. It identifies a problem "like OpenAI." Then proposes solutions insufficient to address it.

Sam Altman told the Federal Reserve in July 2025 that customer support jobs are "totally, totally gone."

TL;DR

  • The displacement is not hypothetical. A call centre worker costs $60,000. GPT-5.4 Mini does the same job for $675. That's an 89:1 cost ratio. At Altman's promised cost trajectory, it reaches $7/year — a 9,000:1 ratio.
  • The fund optimistically delivers $167 per month. A $10 trillion Public Wealth Fund, 34 years in the making, delivers $167 per month per adult. About half a week's groceries.
  • The tax base collapses. 84% of federal revenue depends on labour. A robot tax would need to be 408–1,360% of AI operating costs to replace lost payroll tax alone.
  • The proposals concentrate power, not prosperity. A $10 trillion fund with no elected governance makes the AI supply chain too big to fail and creates a new feudalism.

I. The Displacement Arithmetic

So take him at his word. What does it actually cost to replace a human being, today, using OpenAI's own products?

A U.S. call centre worker costs roughly $60,000/year all-in (salary, benefits, payroll tax, management, training, turnover, workspace). OpenAI's GPT-5 now scores ~97% on Sierra Research's τ²-bench, the standard benchmark for AI customer service agents. In 2024, GPT-4o scored below 50%. The AI does the job.

So what does the replacement cost? A call centre worker handles about 50 interactions per day, 250 working days per year = 12,500 interactions/year. Each interaction averages 3,000 input tokens and 11,500 output/reasoning tokens. Annual token usage: 37.5M input, 143.75M output.

GPT-5.4 Standard ($2.50 per million input / $15 per million output): - 37.5M input tokens × $2.50/M = $94 - 143.75M output and reasoning tokens × $15/M = $2,156 - Annual cost to replace one call centre worker = $2,250

$60,000 versus $2,250. That's a 27:1 cost ratio. And token costs are a fully deductible business expense with no payroll tax, no benefits, no HR overhead.

GPT-5.4 Mini ($0.75/$4.50 per million tokens): - 37.5M × $0.75/M = $28 - 143.75M × $4.50/M = $647 - Annual cost with Mini = $675

89:1. Six hundred and seventy-five dollars versus sixty thousand.

Altman himself has promised this gets worse. In February 2025 he wrote that AI costs drop 10× per year. At a January 2026 town hall he predicted GPT-5.2 level intelligence would become 100× cheaper by end of 2027. If he's even half right, Mini drops to under $7 per year. Seven dollars to replace a $60,000 worker. A 9,000:1 ratio.

Customer service is not an outlier. It is the canary. Anthropic's March 2026 labour market study, based on real Claude usage data across 800+ occupations, found that customer service representatives face 70% observed AI coverage, meaning 70% of their tasks are being performed by AI in professional settings. Computer programmers face similar coverage. The gap between what it can do and what it is doing is enormous.

The gap between the blue and red bars is the displacement that hasn't happened yet. Every cognitive task in that gap is a worker whose position becomes economically untenable as companies discover the 27:1 cost ratio.

U.S. knowledge workers earn roughly $10–11 trillion in annual compensation, over a third of GDP. These are precisely the workers in the 80–96% theoretical AI coverage brackets. The labour share of GDP has fallen to 53.8%, the lowest on record since the BLS began tracking in the 1940s, a decline that has continued through every business cycle since the late 1990s without triggering any policy response.

The Care Economy Trap

The paper presents the care economy as a landing zone for millions of displaced workers. The assumption is that care work is inherently human, AI-resistant, and expandable. Each assumption is wrong.

> What looks like the care economy as a landing zone for displaced workers > Is actually a target. The same cost dynamics that eliminated the call centre worker are coming for every care-adjacent role.

The Economics of Care Work as AI Fallback

Current U.S. care economy employment and the displacement it would need to absorb:

| Sector | Current Employment | |---|---| | Childcare workers | ~990,000 | | Home health aides / personal care | ~4.0 million | | (other care roles) | ... |

Workers with 50%+ task exposure to AI (per OpenAI/UPenn study): ~25 million. Even assuming only 30% are actually displaced within a decade, that's 7.5 million workers needing absorption. The care economy currently employs roughly 69% of that number across all roles combined.

Challenge Magnitude: Average care worker wage (home health aide) ~$35,000/year. Average displaced knowledge worker wage ~$75,000–85,000/year.

Bottom line. Absorbing AI-displaced workers into the care economy requires either a 46–59% wage cut for the displaced workers, or a doubling of the care economy's wage base with no identified funding source.

Altman's own words undermine the care economy thesis. Customer support is the sector he told the Federal Reserve is "totally, totally gone." The OpenAI/UPenn study gives customer service workers 50%+ AI task exposure. So does it for home health aides: 52% theoretical, 27% observed. The care economy is not immune. It is the next wave.

Klarna's experience illustrates the trajectory. In February 2024, Klarna replaced 700 customer service agents with AI. It rehired 300 humans afterwards for quality assurance. The human role that returns after automation is smaller, lower-paid, and supplementary — a quality-assurance layer on top of AI, not a standalone position.

A 2024 meta-analysis in Nature Human Behaviour, analysing 106 experiments with over 370 effect sizes, found that human-AI combinations performed significantly worse than the best of humans or AI alone (effect size g = −0.23). When AI outperformed humans solo, adding a human in the loop produced losses (g = −0.54, a medium effect). These findings reflect current tools and workflows. The dynamic may shift as interaction design improves. But the direction of travel is clear.

But the deeper problem is the trajectory, not the current numbers. The paper assumes care work remains human because it is relational. That assumption has a cost threshold.

Overhead. A human care worker's fully-loaded cost includes not just wages but benefits, training, management, workspace, insurance, and administrative overhead. This overhead doesn't disappear when wages approach zero. It becomes the binding constraint.

This is the Negative Value threshold. The point at which the total cost of human involvement in a workflow exceeds the value of the output that human produces — not because the human is incompetent, but because the system cost of maintaining them exceeds the alternative.

At $60,000 all-in, roughly $40,000 is wages and $20,000 is overhead (management, training, workspace, insurance, HR, compliance). Even if wages fall to zero — even if a worker volunteers — the overhead is still $20,000 per year. The AI does the same job for $675.

The Overhead Trap: When Even Free Labour Destroys Value

Bottom line. Pay the worker nothing. Zero. The overhead of keeping a human in the workflow still costs 30× more than letting AI handle it. $20,000 in overhead versus $675 for GPT-5.4 Mini. This is not "humans are expensive." This is "the system cost of human presence exceeds the system cost of AI presence, even at zero wages."

This is not yet true for deeply relational care work: holding an elderly patient's hand, reading a child a bedtime story, being present for a dying person. These tasks have no AI equivalent. But they are a narrow band. The paper treats them as the entire category.

Care Economy Wage Equilibrium Under Mass Entry

Basic supply/demand. If millions of displaced knowledge workers enter the care economy, what happens to wages?

| Displaced Workers Entering | Supply Increase | Market-Clearing Wage | Wage Change | Workers NOT Absorbed | |---|---|---|---|---| | (various scenarios) | ... | ... | ... | ... |

Bottom line. At 5 million entrants, care wages crash to the minimum wage floor. At 7.5 million, 2.5 million workers find no employment in the care economy at any wage. The care economy absorbs some. It cannot absorb all.

The honest version of the care economy argument is this. In the short term (5–10 years), care work provides partial, lower-wage absorption for a fraction of displaced workers. In the medium term (10–20 years), AI penetration of care tasks will begin, driven by the overhead dynamics described above. In the long term, the care economy is not a solution. It is a delay.

II. The Efficiency Dividend and the Tax Collapse

OpenAI proposes converting AI efficiency gains into shorter workweeks, better benefits, and "predictable prosperity." Specifically: a four-day workweek at the same pay.

They want 32 hours of work for 40 hours of pay. That means you have to cram 100% of the output into 80% of the time.

  • Required hourly output = 1.25× current output
  • Implied productivity jump needed = +25%

So we need a 25% spike in productivity across the board. How fast does that actually happen in the real economy?

  • U.S. labour productivity growth, 2007–2019 average = ~1.4%/year
  • Post-pandemic rate, 2019–2025 = ~2.1%/year
  • Time to hit +25% at those rates = 11 to 17 years

Even with AI acceleration, achieving a 25% productivity boost specifically in labour hours (not just total output) requires that AI substitute for specific tasks within the workday without creating new work to replace them — a condition that has never historically held.

But it gets worse. Acemoglu's recent NBER paper estimates AI-specific productivity gains at roughly 0.05% per year. Time to hit +25% at Acemoglu's rate = 500 years. Even Goldman Sachs' wildly optimistic 7% GDP projection translates to ~0.7% annual productivity growth in labour hours.

The gap between expectation and reality is not theoretical. A February 2026 NBER study of 6,000 executives found 89% reported no productivity change from AI over the past three years. Goldman Sachs calculated that massive AI investment contributed "basically nothing" to productivity growth. A December 2025 Stanford study found workers who adopted AI tools completed tasks more slowly and to worse quality than non-users.

Even OpenAI's own numbers don't support the four-day week. Their December 2025 State of Enterprise AI report, surveying 9,000 workers across nearly 100 organisations, found ChatGPT Enterprise users save 40–60 minutes per day. Heavy users report 10+ hours per week. BCG found AI leaders achieved 1.7× revenue growth. Moderna reported an 8–12% productivity gain. Half of what the four-day week requires. Even the company proposing the policy can't produce evidence its own product delivers the required gains.

Bottom line. The four-day week at same pay requires productivity gains that, at historical rates, would take 11–17 years to materialise. At Acemoglu's AI-specific estimate, 500 years. Meanwhile, 89% of CEOs report zero productivity change from AI. The efficiency dividends have no efficiency to divide. Yet. The paper offers no timeline, no trigger, no mechanism.

The deeper problem is compositional. AI efficiency gains are not distributed uniformly across the economy.

The Distribution Problem: Who Can Actually Go to Four Days?

Mapping the workforce by AI task exposure and estimating plausible productivity gains per bracket (economy-wide weighted average):

| AI Exposure Bracket | Workers | Est. AI Productivity Gain | 4-Day Viable? | |---|---|---|---| | 0–10% exposure | 32M (20%) | ~5% | No | | (other brackets) | ... | ... | ... |

Economy-wide weighted average AI productivity gain: ~22%, below the 25% threshold needed for a four-day week.

Bottom line. The four-day week is feasible for roughly half the workforce — those in high-exposure sectors where AI genuinely multiplies output. For the other half, it requires either a subsidy from the productive sectors or accepting lower output. The paper proposes the policy universally. The arithmetic supports it partially.

There is a harder question the paper never asks. If an AI can do in four minutes what took a knowledge worker four hours, and the company chooses to keep 40 hours of pay while capturing 4× the output, who captures the surplus? The paper assumes workers benefit. Every historical precedent for capital-substituting automation says otherwise.

OpenAI itself concedes the problem. "Workers using AI might well agree that it's increasing their productivity, yet still worry that this won't translate into increased wages or improved working conditions." The paper acknowledges this for current productivity gains, then offers no mechanism to ensure they'll see benefits from future, larger gains.

The four-day week is the optimistic scenario. Assume it works. Now ask who pays for everything else.

The paper correctly identifies that AI will erode the tax base. But it understates how catastrophic the erosion will be.

Individual income taxes, the vast majority derived from wages and salaries, account for 51% of federal revenue. Payroll taxes (Social Security and Medicare) account for another 33%. Together, labour-dependent taxes generate 84% of all federal revenue. Corporate income taxes generate a much smaller share. When AI displaces workers, you don't lose a quarter of the tax base. You lose five-sixths of it. And you lose it simultaneously with a massive increase in demand for safety nets.

OpenAI suggests "higher taxes on capital gains at the top, corporate income, or targeted measures on AI adoption." They call these "robot taxes." The arithmetic doesn't work.

We already did the math on what replacement actually costs. A call centre worker costs $60,000 all-in. GPT-5.4 Standard costs $2,250. The savings are $57,750 per worker.

  • Previous payroll tax base (100 workers × $60K) = $6,000,000
  • Payroll taxes paid (employer + employee, ~15.3%) = $918,000
  • AI replacement cost (100 × $2,250 GPT-5.4 Standard) = $225,000
  • Firm's cost savings = $5,775,000
  • Robot tax needed to replace lost payroll tax = $918,000
  • Robot tax as a percentage of AI operating cost = 408%

Bottom line. To replace lost payroll tax revenue alone, a robot tax would need to be 408% of the AI system's operating cost. A tax four times larger than the thing being taxed. With GPT-5.4 Mini the ratio is 1,360%. And Altman has promised costs drop 10× per year. The robot tax doesn't just create a contradiction — it creates one that compounds annually.

The paper's fallback is conventional. "Higher taxes on capital gains at the top, corporate income, or targeted measures on AI adoption." Each has a fatal flaw: they tax profits. The AI industry is eliminating profit as a concept.

OpenAI is the proof. In 2025 it generated $13 billion in revenue and burned through $8 billion in capital expenditure while posting $14 billion in losses against projected revenue of $29 billion. Gross margins collapsed from 40% in 2024 to 33% in 2025 as compute costs grew faster than revenue. Its valuation exceeds $115 billion. The company does not expect to be cash-flow positive until 2030. It is approaching a trillion-dollar valuation while structurally unprofitable.

It's not just AI companies. Every company that adopts AI can convert profit margin into compute spend — reducing taxable income while increasing output. This is not avoidance. It is the rational economic response to a world where intelligence is an operating expense.

> What looks like tax-advantaged infrastructure investment > Is actually a public subsidy to destroy the public tax base. The entire logic of accelerated depreciation and investment credits assumes capital stock creates jobs. A $500M data centre employs 50 people. A $500M factory floor employs 5,000.

The paper proposes that "AI data centres should pay their own way on energy so that households aren't subsidising them." The arithmetic makes this claim almost quaint.

Industrial electricity rates range from $0.054/kWh in New Mexico to $0.15/kWh in the Northeast (EIA, 2025). For a 1 GW data centre consuming 8.76 TWh per year, the spread between Texas and Virginia is roughly $88 million. Meanwhile, state data centre tax breaks cost $3.1 billion per year across just ten states (Good Jobs First, April 2025), and 21 other states don't report their numbers. Virginia alone waived $1.6–1.9 billion in FY2025 on a tax break originally projected to cost $1.54 million per year when passed in 2008 — an 85% property tax discount. One Microsoft data centre in Illinois received $38 million in exemptions and created 20 permanent jobs. Georgia found that 70% of data centre construction would have occurred without the breaks. And OpenAI itself received $400M in federal and state subsidies for Stargate.

"Pay their own way on energy" is a debate about $88 million per gigawatt. The tax subsidy is $3 billion and growing. The paper proposes closing the wrong gap.

The game theory has a name, and a recent precedent.

In April 2020, oil went to negative $37 a barrel. Producers were paying people to take crude off their hands. Why? Because shutting down a well is more expensive than paying someone to take the oil. The market structure was Stackelberg competition with a competitive fringe. Saudi Arabia was the Stackelberg leader. US shale was the fringe. The fringe couldn't shut down fast enough to avoid the price collapse.

AI intelligence is following the same curve. Sam Altman promises 10× cost drops per year. The competitive fringe — the displaced human workers — must accept the leader's price or exit.

> What looks like market competition driving down prices > Is actually Stackelberg predation eliminating the competitive fringe. The same structure that drove oil to -$37/barrel is driving the value of human cognitive labour toward zero.

The fundamental error is taxing flows rather than taxing stocks. When AI replaces labour, the savings flow to capital owners. Those savings compound. The resulting wealth concentrates in the stock of accumulated wealth and the rent on scarce assets (which discourages hoarding without discouraging production).

The urgency is quantifiable. The paper identifies the erosion but doesn't model it.

Tax Base Erosion: How Fast Is the Floor Disappearing?

Labour-dependent taxes (individual income tax + payroll) currently generate roughly $4.4 trillion per year, 84% of total federal revenue. Under AI displacement scenarios, the erosion is massive.

| Scenario | Labour Tax Erosion (10-yr) | Annual Revenue Lost | |---|---|---| | Slow (1%/yr labour share decline) | 9.6% | $421B | | (Moderate and Fast scenarios) | ... | ... |

Impact on Social Security (OASDI currently runs a $67B/year deficit against a $2,720B trust fund):

Bottom line. Under the moderate scenario, consistent with the paper's own characterisation of the transition, the U.S. would lose nearly $800 billion per year in labour-dependent revenue within a decade. Social Security's crisis arrives on roughly the same schedule, compressed by 5–7 years from the SSA's 2033 baseline.

A coherent tax reform for the intelligence age would follow the paper's own logic to its conclusion.

III. The Public Wealth Fund

The paper's headline proposal is a Public Wealth Fund that gives every citizen a stake in AI-driven productivity growth. The concept is appealing. Sovereign wealth fund proposals have existed for decades. The question is always the same.

Where does the seed capital come from, and how large must it be to matter?

Public Wealth Fund: What Would It Take?

The U.S. adult population is approximately 267 million. To provide a meaningful annual dividend, say $2,000 per adult per year:

| Assumed Return | Required Fund Size | |---|---| | 7% nominal (optimistic equity) | $7.6 trillion | | 5% nominal (blended portfolio) | $10.7 trillion | | 4% nominal (conservative) | $13.4 trillion |

For context, the total U.S. federal budget is approximately $7.0 trillion. Norway's sovereign wealth fund — the world's largest — holds approximately $1.7 trillion.

Bottom line. To distribute $2,000 per adult per year, the U.S. would need a fund 3.5–6× the size of the world's largest sovereign wealth fund. The paper proposes this without specifying a source.

$2,000 per adult per year is $167 per month. About half a week's groceries. That is what a $10 trillion fund, 34 years in the making, ultimately delivers. The paper presents this as prosperity. The arithmetic presents it as subsistence arithmetic with a 34-year lag.

The Timeline Gap: When the Fund Arrives vs. When It's Needed

Even if the fund were seeded immediately, compound growth takes decades to reach adequate scale. Meanwhile, displacement is already accelerating.

| Seeding Rate | Years to Reach $10T (at 6% return) | |---|---| | $50B/year | 45 years | | $100B/year (1.5% of federal budget) | ~34 years | | (Other rates) | ... |

Anthropic's data shows 70–96% theoretical AI coverage across cognitive occupations, with observed coverage already at 27–70%. The displacement is not future tense.

Bottom line. At $100B/year seeding, the fund reaches adequate scale in Year 34. Displacement peaks within a decade. The fund arrives a generation after the crisis it is designed to address. A pension plan for the grandchildren of the displaced.

The timeline contradiction is made explicit by OpenAI's own predictions. The company expects superintelligence — by which it means AI that can do most cognitive work better than most humans — within "a few years." The fund requires 34. These numbers are not reconcilable.

Asked how the fund would be seeded, the paper offers: "Policymakers and AI companies should work together." The vagueness is not incidental. The mechanics are impossible to specify because they don't exist.

But there is a deeper structural problem the paper does not acknowledge. A $10 trillion fund investing in "AI-driven growth" must primarily invest in the AI supply chain. There is no other market segment of comparable scale. The AI ecosystem's market cap puts this in stark relief.

The market cap arithmetic makes this concrete. The current AI ecosystem (NVIDIA at $4.4 trillion, Apple at $3.3 trillion, Microsoft at $3.0 trillion, Google at $2.0 trillion, Meta at $1.7 trillion, OpenAI at $1.0T–1.5T implied, plus the supply chain) is approximately $22.5 trillion. If the fund concentrates in AI companies, as the paper's "share in AI-driven growth" framing implies, the fund would need to acquire 45% of the current AI ecosystem to reach $10 trillion. That is a controlling position.

Alternatively, the AI ecosystem would need to triple to ~$71 trillion so the fund could hold 14% and stay a minority holder. That requires the AI sector to become larger than the current entire U.S. equity market.

The dividend model fails on its own terms. The fund distributes returns. Returns require profitable companies. The AI industry's structural dynamics — Stackelberg competition, margin compression, the race to intelligence-at-zero-cost — eliminate the profits that generate the returns that fund the dividends.

Even avoiding direct investment in the labs, the supply chain makes escape impossible. NVIDIA, Microsoft, Amazon, Google — the AI ecosystem IS the equity market at this point. You cannot "share in AI-driven growth" without owning the AI supply chain.

Even the minority stake misidentifies what matters. In the intelligence age, the means of production is not factories or land — it is the model weights, the training pipelines, the inference infrastructure, and above all the data. A minority stake in the companies that own these assets gives you a share of their returns. It gives you no voice in how they are used.

> What looks like shared ownership > Is actually a minority stake in someone else's engine. The public gets a dividend. The AI companies keep the training data, the model weights, the deployment infrastructure, and the decision-making power. The fund is a permanent minority investor in the companies that are eliminating the tax base that funds the public services the fund is meant to supplement.

A fund that buys a minority position in the market with public money and distributes the dividends is a mechanism for laundering public capital into private hands while calling it redistribution.

Worse, the fund is a trap. It has four jaws.

First, it is a forced buyer. To reach $10 trillion, the fund must place bids under every company in the AI supply chain for decades. This suppresses volatility — exactly the Stackelberg predation that drives the price of intelligence below the cost of human participation. The fund finances the weapon that displaces the workers it is designed to protect.

Second, the fund makes the AI supply chain too big to fail. Once a sovereign wealth fund holds significant positions across NVIDIA, Microsoft, OpenAI, Google, and the rest of the AI stack, no government can regulate those companies without reducing the fund's returns — reducing the dividends to citizens. The fund becomes a lobbyist for its holdings. Every dollar of public investment in AI companies is a dollar lobbying against AI regulation.

Third, fiduciary duty runs to the majority shareholders, not the minority ones. Company leadership and governance remains with existing shareholders. The fund's minority stakes give it no board seats, no veto rights, no policy influence — only the residual claim on distributed profits that the structural dynamics of AI competition will progressively eliminate.

Fourth, even purpose-built governance cannot constrain the people actually in charge. A New Yorker investigation published the same week as OpenAI's policy paper, based on over 100 interviews and previously undisclosed documents, documented how OpenAI's board restructuring has progressively consolidated decision-making power in its CEO while creating governance structures that appear independent but are not.

The Fund as described redistributes investment returns without changing what produces them. It skims a fraction of the surplus from a system that is concentrating the rest. It does not change the structure. It ratifies it.

The paper's deepest failure is not economic but political. Every proposal in the document, taken together, would construct a system of political and social power unlike anything in democratic history. The paper never examines this. It should be its central concern.

Consider what the proposals actually construct. A Public Wealth Fund of the required scale ($7–13 trillion) with no elected governance, controlled by or adjacent to the executive branch, invested primarily in the companies that are eliminating the labour tax base that funds democracy itself. An AI infrastructure built through public subsidy ($400M+ to OpenAI directly, $3B+ in annual state tax breaks) with no public ownership of the resulting assets. A care economy transition that concentrates the displaced workforce in low-wage, high-dependency roles. A four-day workweek proposal that offers workers a share of current productivity gains while reserving for corporations the option to capture future gains.

The Fund as Political Actor

At $10 trillion, the Public Wealth Fund's ownership stakes would dwarf existing institutional investors.

| Entity | Assets Under Management | |---|---| | Norway Government Pension Fund (world's largest sovereign fund) | ~$2.2 trillion | | BlackRock (world's largest asset manager) | ~$11.6 trillion | | Proposed U.S. Public Wealth Fund | $7–13 trillion |

At $10 trillion invested across diversified U.S. and global equities, the Fund would hold roughly 14% of total U.S. equity market cap. In the AI supply chain specifically — the sector it is designed to target — the concentration would be substantially higher.

Bottom line. A single government-adjacent entity would become one of the largest shareholders in most major American corporations. This is not redistribution. It is state capitalism without state accountability.

The Net Effect: A New Feudalism?

Sum the proposals. A handful of companies control the cognitive layer (foundation models). A government-adjacent fund holds minority stakes in those companies. Workers have "portable benefits" and "voice mechanisms" — consultative rights with no structural power. The public receives $167/month in dividends, 34 years from now.

The result is a techno-feudal arrangement in which the lords own the intelligence, the crown owns the land (or rather, the equity), and the serfs receive a subsistence allotment. The paper never names this structure. It calls it "shared prosperity."

The one group whose interests are structurally last in every proposal: the displaced worker. Last to know, last to decide, last to benefit from the structural changes that reshape their lives.

The Verdict: Benevolent Intentions, Feudal Architecture

OpenAI's paper wants a future where AI amplifies human purpose rather than replacing it, where abundance is shared rather than concentrated. These are the right values. The architecture delivers the opposite.

What to keep.

The portable benefits concept. The adaptive safety nets. The worker voice proposals. The instinct that transition costs should be shared. The four-day week as an aspiration, with honest math about the timeline. The Public Wealth Fund as a concept, with honest math about the scale and governance it would require.

What to fix.

The Public Wealth Fund needs democratic governance, controlling stakes not minority positions, and revenue sourced from wealth stocks not productivity flows. The robot tax needs to be understood as arithmetically impossible at current AI cost trajectories and replaced with a wealth tax and land value tax. The care economy proposal needs to grapple with the overhead dynamics and wage equilibria that will make it a partial delay, not a solution. The four-day week needs a trigger mechanism tied to measured productivity gains, not aspirational projections.

What to confront.

Others have argued that the paper misreads the political history it invokes. This piece shows it also misreads the economics it claims to have done. The proposals are not wrong because they are malicious. They are wrong because they are proposals by a company that benefits from the transition it is describing.

Every proposal should be tested against a single question:

Who holds power, and how can they be held accountable?

The distance between OpenAI's values and its proposals is not hypocrisy. It is the predictable result of asking the entity that benefits most from displacement to design the response to it.

The arithmetic is the same in every future. The architecture determines which one we get.
