Sulphuric Acid, Naphtha and Super El Niño Polycrisis

By @ctindale — Apr 23, 2026

War, El Niño, Pestilence, and Famine: The Coming Shock to Global Food Supplies

The global agricultural and industrial complex is heading into a poly shock, with an upstream sulphuric acid and naphtha supply collapse colliding with a probable super El Niño event to create an asymmetric, compounding food security crisis.

Sulphuric acid is a critical upstream chokepoint; approximately 45% of global consumption is dedicated to phosphate fertiliser production. Without it, phosphate rock cannot be converted into soluble plant-available forms.

This chemical bottleneck is currently being compressed by the near-total closure of the Strait of Hormuz, which has disrupted sea-borne sulphur and pyrite shipments, while simultaneously depriving global refineries of the smelter by-product acid supply.

India has instituted domestic prioritisation for its fertiliser producers amid annual sulphuric acid imports of approximately 2.4 million metric tons.

This input-side supply collapse coincides with strong atmospheric teleconnections: meteorological conditions are evolving in a manner consistent with a historical 1877-78 magnitude event.

The convergence of higher nutrient application costs and climatic stress guarantees widespread yield reductions across three or four of the world's primary agricultural belts simultaneously.

Significant declines in crop yields are shaping up to be severe. The secondary impacts, such as food price inflation in chronic food importers, sovereign balance-of-payments stress, and Panama Canal capacity disruption, compound the primary agricultural shock.

Plants, Fertiliser and El Niño. How they interact.

Next year's agricultural crisis is a trap in which a global sulphuric acid and naphtha shortage collides with the biological moment that plants most need those chemicals.

Most people assume a thirsty plant needs less fertiliser, but to survive heat, crops actually need...

They need phosphorus to give them the energy to quickly grow deep roots to chase receding underground water tables.

These nutrients are naturally locked inside hard rocks and can only be made digestible for plants by dissolving phosphate rock in sulphuric acid.

Without this acid, farmers are cornered with very few viable workarounds. They can't just dump heavy loads of nitrogen to compensate and they can't simply ship in ready-made phosphate because the shipping industry and the ports that process bulk fertiliser are already under stress from Hormuz rerouting.

They also can't compensate by simply using cheap nitrogen, because excess nitrogen tricks the plant into lush above-ground growth while abandoning root development, which is precisely the opposite of what stressed crops need during a hot and dry El Niño season.

Farming is about formulas and processes; you can't just skip parts without consequences. Most of the commercial world runs on just-in-time supply chains, so there isn't a buffer.

Ultimately, because the world is missing a single industrial acid, we are stripping our crops of the exact nutrient combination they need during the worst possible climatic moment.

So the timing of this poly crisis is really poor. There is no worse time to run out of fertiliser than a super El Niño, and yet the forecasts are epic.

Pestilence, Herbicide and Pesticide, and how they are even a bigger risk to Food Security

Public discussion tends to linger on natural gas and the Haber-Bosch process, as if fertiliser were the only agricultural input under threat. But the Hormuz blockade has simultaneously severed naphtha flows, the petrochemical feedstock from which almost every herbicide, insecticide, and fungicide in commercial use is derived.

Those chemicals form the foundation of the recipe for pesticides and herbicides, without which modern high-yield agriculture is not possible.

Roughly 1.2 million barrels per day of naphtha move through Hormuz, equal to about 48 million metric tons annually, representing approximately 20% of globally traded naphtha.

A supply chain breakdown might begin in the refineries and steam crackers, pass through aromatic solvents, and then terminate in the field as pesticide application rates collapse.

The system is concentrated, geographically and technically, and because East Asia sits at the point of maximum exposure in the global naphtha web, the shock will radiate outward from there with extraordinary speed.

China, Japan, South Korea, and Taiwan absorb the bulk of these naphtha flows, with more than 60 per cent of total regional demand met by seaborne imports from the Gulf, and these nations also host the vast majority of global active-ingredient manufacturing capacity for agrochemicals.

The idea that a deficit can be patched over by improvisation collapses under inspection: bypass pipelines do not exist at any meaningful scale, alternative feedstocks require retooling of crackers and reformers that cannot be accomplished in a single season, and specialty chemical inventory chains carry only weeks of forward cover.

Asian crackers and reformers, which usually carry less than a month of feedstock inventory, therefore face near-immediate curtailment decisions.

The consequence is a blow to the two processes that sustain the aromatics pool: catalytic reforming of naphtha for BTX production and pyrolysis in steam crackers.

Once both channels are starved, the world loses roughly 15 to 19 million metric tons of BTX, an immediate 18 to 24 per cent reduction in global aromatics output.

The crisis is not confined to abstract feedstock scarcity but runs straight through the practical question of whether pesticides can be produced and delivered to farms in time for the growing season.

Benzene is the underlying molecular scaffold from which chlorobenzene, aniline, and nitrobenzene are synthesised, and without these intermediates the entire downstream pesticide sequence stalls.

In many ways, this is a more critical bottleneck than fertiliser because pure active ingredients are genuinely irreplaceable in the short run.

These are the solvents that keep emulsifiable concentrates in solution, let the spray remain on the leaf surface, and enable active ingredients to penetrate plant cuticles and insect exoskeletons.

Without them, pesticides cease to function as usable agricultural tools. The usual fantasy of substituting bio-pesticides, essential oils, or mechanical controls at scale collapses when you examine the economics and the throughput required to protect billions of hectares of commercial farmland on a timeline of months.

Once that chemical protection wears off, the biological logic follows.

Modern crops have been bred under conditions of intensive chemical defence, meaning they carry the yield genetics of protected plants, not wild-type resistance to pests, weeds, and fungi.

Remove herbicides, insecticides, and fungicides, and the field won't become less efficient; it reverts toward pre-Green Revolution baselines of biological competition.

This reveals some yield-loss baselines, 34 per cent potential destruction to weeds, 18 per cent to insect pests, and 16 per cent to plant diseases in a no-protection scenario.

Maize, with a global baseline of about 1.23 billion metric tons, loses 45 per cent of its value, erasing more than half a billion metric tons of calorie output.

The calory math is harsh: 1,970 trillion kilocalories vanish with maize, 1,334 trillion with wheat, and 1,530 trillion with rice under a no-pesticide scenario; equivalent to the caloric needs of roughly 500 million people for an entire year.

Some of the missing naphtha could be displaced by ethane, propane, butane/LPG, alternative liquid feedstocks, and recycled plastics in crackers, which would shift the product slate away from aromatics toward lighter olefins. China's import of ethane from the US has skyrocketed.

But none of those routes reproduces, on short notice and at a global scale, the same volume and product mix that naphtha delivers for aromatics.

Commodity markets will price in future scarcity immediately, governments will act on anticipated shortfalls rather than confirmed ones, and panic buying, export restrictions, and logistical crowding will compound the physical shortage into something more severe.

China would harden seasonal fertiliser controls into outright export bans, Russia and Belarus will redirect potash toward domestic allies, and India will redirect subsidised urea exclusively toward domestic consumption. These are not risks; they are already visible policy postures accelerating.

Brazil is presented as the worst case because it combines immense agricultural output with a dependence on imported nutrients, a deep exposure to BTX-derived chemicals, and significant El Niño sensitivity in its most productive zones.

For chronic food importers such as Egypt, Indonesia, and the Philippines, the shock becomes a balance-of-payments crisis layered on a humanitarian one, because rising import costs for both food and inputs will collide with weakening export revenues and currency depreciation.

A Hormuz naphtha blockade is a famine mechanism camouflaged as a shipping disruption, because once the molecules stop moving, there is no technical fix inside a single growing season.

BTX-derived herbicides, insecticides, fungicides and heavy aromatic solvents keep modern monoculture farms biologically viable. Remove them, and the crop genetics tuned for maximum chemical-protected yields simply collapse toward pre-industrial loss rates that the world's current population cannot sustain.

El Niño's impacts are geographically uneven, though uniformly catastrophic, yet they are large enough to sever the final margin of safety for food-importing nations that are already running structural deficits.

WMO notes that El Niño typically brings severe drought to Australia, Indonesia and parts of southern Africa and accelerates cyclone formation in the central Pacific. These effects reduce agricultural output precisely in the regions where alternative sourcing is least flexible.

In practical terms, that means heat stress, water stress and disordered planting calendars raise the per-hectare nutrient requirement at the exact moment that sulphuric acid and naphtha constraints are forcing down the per-hectare application of those nutrients and chemicals.

The interaction is therefore multiplicative: El Niño won't create a separate crisis running alongside the supply crisis but will instead amplify the supply shock's biological effects, pushing yield losses beyond what either stressor would achieve on its own.

Remember I'm not trying to predict what will unfold here because it's too complex to call and a doom scenario is not guaranteed. I am simply highlighting the convergence of risk and showing how individually significant supply and climate risks interact to create combined effects far more severe than any single factor can explain.

Primary Compounding Mechanisms

The severity of this polycrisis stems from the simultaneous nature of the supply and climate shocks, which interact across several distinct but interconnected agricultural and industrial mechanisms.

The interplay of chemical scarcity, biological requirements, and global logistics creates a landscape in which individual risk factors do not simply add together but multiply across each other.

Reduced Application Rates vs. Heightened Per-Hectare Nutrient Demand

Under normal climatic conditions, commercial crops possess a baseline nutrient requirement to achieve target yields. However, under extreme weather stress, such as the severe drought and heat anticipated across the Western Hemisphere and parts of Asia due to the forthcoming El Niño event, that per-hectare nutrient requirement doesn't stay the same; it increases substantially, often by 15 to 30 per cent above baseline.

The Food and Agriculture Organisation (FAO) notes that granular urea prices in the Middle East spiked 147% in 2022, and phosphate rose 270% during the same period following the Russia-Ukraine conflict, triggering significant under-application across African and South Asian farms.

Faced with these ruinous economics, farmers are forced to drastically reduce application rates per hectare, often cutting 30 to 50 per cent below recommended agronomic optima, at the precise moment crops require elevated nutrient inputs.

Timing Mismatches with Planting Windows

Modern agricultural supply chains are rigidly dictated by inflexible biological clocks. In regions like sub-Saharan Africa and Southeast Asia, critical planting windows operate within three to four week windows tied to monsoonal cycles.

The geopolitical necessity of rerouting maritime trade around the Cape of Good Hope, bypassing the Red Sea and Suez Canal, has added 10 to 14 days to freight journeys to European ports, with even greater delays for Asian and East African destinations.

Even if a physical product is secured at a premium, this delayed delivery frequently results in the agricultural product arriving outside of the critical biological application window.

Price and Logistics Spirals

The systemic nature of the disruption has ignited a self-reinforcing logistics and cost spiral that has structural parallels to the 2021–22 supply chain crisis, but operating under tighter underlying fundamentals.

Furthermore, war-risk insurance premiums for vessels transiting adjacent high-risk zones have surged sharply, adding further cost inflation to freight rates.

Ag producers face a triple-inflationary bind: the underlying commodity price of the fertiliser has dramatically escalated; freight and insurance costs have surged; and critical planting-window urgency forces buyers to accept spot premiums.

It was already a marginal business for many.

Political Allocation Dynamics (Fertiliser vs. Pure Mining)

As sulphuric acid rapidly transitions from an abundant, cheap industrial chemical to a critically scarce strategic resource, a fierce domestic political struggle is erupting between the fertiliser industry and the mining sector for priority allocation of available supplies.

China's decision to implement a comprehensive halt on smelter by-product sulphuric acid exports from May 2026 is the most consequential policy signal in global agricultural commodity markets in decades.

Beijing has chosen to prioritise domestic fertiliser manufacturing and agricultural autonomy over the global agricultural system, acknowledging this as a national strategic asset.

In resource-rich developing nations like Indonesia and those in Central Africa, intense domestic political debates are emerging over whether to prioritise sulphuric acid allocation to the mining sector for critical mineral extraction (copper, nickel, lithium), which generates vital export revenues and foreign exchange, or to the agricultural sector.

This political allocation weaponises the reagent supply chain, transferring the shortage directly onto the most vulnerable global food importers.

Third-Order & Polycrisis Amplifiers

The interaction of these primary mechanisms causes a cascade of third-order amplifiers that propagate the initial shock across the broader global economy, with implications that extend well beyond immediate food price inflation.

Water–Fertiliser Interaction ("Double Nutrient Loss")

Climate models indicate that the impending super El Niño will distribute extreme weather asymmetrically, creating conditions of severe drought in some zones and intense flooding in others.

Both extremes critically undermine fertiliser efficacy, creating a phenomenon of "double nutrient loss."

Without sufficient soil moisture, applied fertilisers cannot dissolve and enter the root system, stranding expensive nutrients in the soil surface in a crystalline state.

Conversely, in regions experiencing El Niño-induced flooding (such as the South American Pampas), excess rainfall rapidly leaches soluble nutrients from the root zone, flushing them into drainage systems before crops can assimilate them.

This results in severe agricultural runoff, triggering downstream eutrophication of waterways, while crops are simultaneously deprived of nutrients despite the physical application.

Fisheries–Livestock–Fertiliser Loop

The El Niño oceanic anomaly fundamentally alters deep-water upwelling, particularly along the western coast of South America, with profound and immediate consequences for global fisheries, livestock, and organic nutrient supply chains.

Recognising the imminent threat of warming coastal waters, the Peruvian Production Ministry (PRODUCE) has already begun implementing preemptive restrictions on the anchoveta fishing season, with early data suggesting Total Allowable Catch (TAC) reductions of up to 40 per cent compared to prior years.

Because the Peruvian anchoveta is the foundational species for global fishmeal and fish oil production, supplying approximately 65–70% of the global fishmeal market, these TAC reductions have immediate and severe cascading effects throughout global aquaculture and livestock production, particularly in East Asia.

Fishmeal by-products are highly sought after as premium organic fertilisers and soil conditioners. Their sudden scarcity further constrains the already critically tight global nutrient supply.

Multi-Year Soil Nutrient Mining Legacy and 2028–2030 Yield Hangover

When farmers are forced to apply insufficient fertiliser due to exorbitant costs, crops instinctively draw upon their internal reserves of nutrients stored in the soil organic matter built up over years or even decades of cultivation.

Agronomic models demonstrate that continuous nutrient mining rapidly degrades soil organic carbon content and fundamentally alters soil microbial communities.

Consequently, the extreme under-application forced by the 2026–2027 crisis guarantees a persistent "yield hangover" effect for potentially three to five years beyond the immediate crisis period.

This situation caps maximum theoretical yields globally, regardless of whether weather patterns normalise and supply chains recover.

Energy-Transition Double-Bind

Sulphuric acid is the critical solvent required for hydrometallurgical leaching across the critical minerals industry, which is the industrial backbone of the global energy transition.

The current reagent shortage has already triggered severe bottlenecks in the supply chains essential for copper, nickel, lithium, cobalt, and uranium production.

In Indonesia, which accounts for over 60% of global nickel production via acid-intensive High-Pressure Acid Leach (HPAL) processes, supply curtailments are generating a cascade throughout the global EV battery supply chain.

Kazakhstan's Kazatomprom, the world's dominant uranium producer utilising In-Situ Recovery (ISR) technology, is also facing severe supply constraints directly tied to the reagent shortage.

Concurrently, El Niño-driven droughts routinely cripple hydroelectric power generation, the primary energy source for a significant proportion of critical mineral refining and processing operations.

Just when it's experiencing significant new demand.

Asymmetric Macro Winners/Losers

The dual shock fundamentally restructures the global commodities hierarchy, creating market asymmetries that will persist well beyond the immediate crisis.

Conversely, fully integrated, captive-acid producers with vertically integrated supply chains, such as Mosaic Company, OCP Group (Morocco), and the Russian fertiliser conglomerates, gain enormous market power and pricing influence.

Geopolitically, nations capable of circumventing blockades, such as Iran, are capitalising on the chaos by offering discounted inputs to geopolitically non-aligned buyers.

Though Iran's advantage is most likely very short-lived.

Insurance/Reinsurance Capacity Crunch and Spillovers

Global agribusiness is underpinned by complex insurance and reinsurance mechanisms, which are currently facing an acute capacity crunch in the face of escalating, geographically correlated, and systemic risks.

The 2026 super El Niño, arriving on top of historic asset accumulation in hazard-prone areas, is pushing agricultural reinsurance markets toward a critical inflection point.

The US Federal Crop Insurance Program (FCIP) has seen record liability protection of $159.3 billion in 2023, reflecting an exponential growth in insured agricultural asset values and premiums.

In response to these non-diversifiable, systemic climate shocks, major reinsurers are tightening underwriting criteria, dramatically increasing deductibles, and radically repricing risk, particularly for geographically concentrated and climate-exposed agricultural sectors.

This capacity crunch forces agribusinesses to accept higher financial risk or transition to alternative risk management strategies, ultimately flowing through to farm-gate prices and consumer food costs.

Geopolitical "Fertiliser Diplomacy", Export Bans, and Migration Triggers

As domestic food security dominates national security agendas, global trade is fragmenting via aggressive export restrictions and bilateral preferential agreements, fundamentally undermining the multilateral trading system.

This environment effectively weaponises agricultural inputs. The secondary effect is devastating for the most vulnerable global food importers, such as sub-Saharan Africa, the Middle East and North Africa.

The World Food Programme warns that the compounding shocks could push an additional 45 million people into acute food insecurity in the most exposed regions.

The collapse of local agricultural economies serves as a primary push-factor, highly likely to trigger significant and sustained waves of climate-driven and food-insecurity-driven migration.

Innovation Acceleration

Systemic crises often drive technological adoption. In response to the 2026 reagent and climate shocks, a wave of agricultural technology innovation is being fast-tracked.

Precision agriculture technologies, utilising satellite spatial mapping, variable-rate fertilisation algorithms, and AI-driven crop monitoring, are enabling farmers in the most productive and technologically advanced regions to optimise and maximise the agronomic efficiency of severely constrained fertiliser inputs.

They will significantly reduce herbicide usage globally over the next decade. Concurrently, adoption of next-generation weed-control technologies, such as I-Terra, is accelerating.

Till then, though, closure of Hormuz means a contraction in global agricultural yields by physically stranding the molecules that make crop protection possible.

Modern agrochemistry is a direct extension of Middle Eastern energy extraction, relying entirely on the naphtha that flows through that chokepoint to produce the herbicides, insecticides, and fungicides without which monoculture farming cannot sustain its current yields.

Stripping millions of tons of intermediate chemical capacity from the market strands pre-refined precursors at origin and forces the aromatics chain into a cascade of substitution failures.

This sudden deficit in physical plant capacity removes the chemical mechanisms required to eliminate yield-reducing weeds, insects, and pathogens across hundreds of millions of hectares of commercial farmland.

In the industrial sector, multi-billion-dollar investments are being fast-tracked into circular economy solutions, sulphur recovery optimisation and alternative acid production pathways.

However, this rapid technological shift risks leaving undercapitalised smallholder farmers behind, potentially exacerbating inequalities within and between nations.

Panama Canal Logistics Chokepoint

The logistics network supporting global food transport is a climate-driven chokepoint that threatens to compound the supply shock with a distribution shock.

The impending El Niño event threatens to drastically reduce rainfall in the Panamanian watershed. NOAA forecasts already indicate an 80% probability that Lake Gatun will drop below historically critical operational levels by mid-2026.

If transits are throttled, as witnessed during the 2023–24 drought, when daily transits plummeted to a critical low of 18, global food commodity shipping and freight rates will face significant upward pressure.

This bottleneck amplifies global freight competition, strands inventory at sea, and introduces extreme unpredictability into logistics planning.

Quantifiable Risk Scenarios

Based on current data aggregates, historical shock modelling, and leading economic indicators, the following quantitative risk scenarios are assessed.

Global Fertiliser Availability Shortfall

  • Baseline Scenario: A 12–15% global deficit in phosphate and nitrogen availability. This is driven by China's firm removal of acid from global export markets, ongoing Hormuz disruptions, and India's domestic prioritisation mandates.
  • Severe Scenario: A 20–25% absolute deficit. If the Strait of Hormuz remains entirely closed past Q3 2026, the ongoing geopolitical conflict results in escalating secondary sanctions and insurance restrictions, and if Russia and Belarus implement broad potash export quotas, this severe shortfall scenario becomes plausible.

Yield Impacts on Key Commodities

  • Maize: 10–15% yield reduction in heavily exposed regions (such as Southern Africa and Central America) due to the combined stress of inadequate fertiliser application and intense El Niño-driven drought. On a global basis, this represents a reduction of 120–185 million metric tons.
  • Wheat: 15% aggregate yield reduction in Australia, with highly variable impacts across the US Plains, heavily dependent on the temporal intensity of El Niño during the critical spring planting period.
  • Rice: 5–10% global yield suppression. While some specific Southeast Asian basins may see marginal gains, severe reductions in South Asia and sub-Saharan African rice production are heavily weighted to the downside.
  • Palm Oil: 10–20% yield reduction in Indonesia and Malaysia. This mirrors historical precedents from the 1997 "Godzilla" El Niño event and is highly likely given current forecast trajectories.

2027 Food-Price Inflation Range

Global institutions are already tracking the early tremors of this inflation cycle. The FAO Food Price Index registered its highest reading since May 2023 in early 2026, and forward-curve commodity markets are beginning to price in supply-side risk premiums.

If the dual shock holds its current trajectory, models project global fertiliser prices will average 35–45% above the 2024 baseline, with sharp price acceleration concentrated in the Q3–Q4 2026 purchasing and planting period.

In emerging markets, local food inflation could spike by 15–25% year-on-year, driven by the compound effect of currency depreciation, elevated import costs, and domestic supply shortfalls.

Balance-of-Payments Pressure

Emerging markets that are structurally reliant on open-market grain and fertiliser imports (e.g., Egypt, Ethiopia, Bangladesh, the Philippines, Indonesia, and several West African nations) face severe balance-of-payments pressure.

Sovereign capital reserves will be rapidly depleted as governments attempt to subsidise basic nutrient and food imports to prevent civil unrest.

Strategic Implications

The synthesis of deep geoeconomic supply fragmentation and super El Niño climate risk dictates a high probability of a severe polycrisis unfolding across the 2026–2027 agricultural cycle, with ramifications extending into 2028 and beyond.

Food-Security Hot Spots and Humanitarian Flashpoints

The Sahel, West and Central Africa, and the broader Middle East will unquestionably emerge as the epicentres of the global food security crisis.

Winners and Losers: Integrated vs. Merchant Producers

The era of just-in-time, merchant-reliant chemical procurement is functionally over. Vertically integrated agro-industrial conglomerates with captive acid and chemical production, extensive agricultural land holdings across diverse geographies, and robust political connections to nutrient-surplus nations will emerge from this period significantly stronger.

Ripple Effects: Copper, Nickel, Uranium, and Green-Tech

The global energy transition is structurally dependent on the continuous, cheap supply of sulphuric acid as a critical reagent. The current shortfall is placing unprecedented strain on the supply chains of copper, nickel, cobalt, lithium, and uranium.

Policy and Market Signals

Sovereign actors will increasingly utilise blunt trade controls to manage domestic inflation. The trend toward bilateral and preferential trade agreements for agricultural commodities and inputs will dramatically accelerate.

While the convergence of wartime chokepoints, sulphuric-acid and naphtha shortages, and a probable super El Niño creates a daunting set of compounding risks, a total collapse of global agricultural systems is not a predetermined outcome.

History demonstrates that when confronted with severe input and climate shocks, markets, farmers, and governments do adapt and respond, albeit with significant and unavoidable time lags.

Vertically integrated producers and certain surplus nations will likely weather the period far better than fragmented, import-dependent economies.

The downside risks to yields, food security, and critical mineral supply chains are materially elevated and underpriced by most market participants.

The ultimate severity will be determined by both the intensity and duration of the shocks and by the speed and effectiveness of adaptive responses across all levels of the global agricultural and geopolitical system.

What makes the coming polycrisis uniquely dangerous is its near-perfect synchronisation.

A strong El Niño imposes heat and drought on crops; it also sharply increases their biological requirement for phosphorus and other nutrients that sulphuric acid helps to deliver.

Under extreme weather stress, plants need higher levels of phosphorus to drive deep root growth toward receding water tables, potassium to control their water use efficiency under heat load, and sulphur to synthesise the proteins that sustain their structural integrity.

Yet the sulphuric-acid bottleneck is restricting fertiliser production while the naphtha/BTX shortage is disabling crop protection chemistry at precisely the same moment.

This creates a destructive mismatch: peak biological demand colliding with constrained chemical supply during the worst possible climatic window.

Watch List: Leading Indicators

To confirm or mitigate this polycrisis trajectory over the next 6 to 18 months, strategic risk desks should monitor the following leading indicators with disciplined rigour.

  • NOAA Niño-3.4 SST Anomalies: A sustained temperature reading above +2.0°C by August/September 2026 will mathematically confirm the super El Niño designation and validate the most severe agricultural impact models.
  • China Customs Export Data: Complete enforcement of the May 2026 sulphuric acid export halt is the baseline expectation. Any significant volume leakage, driven by political necessity or revenue pressure, would represent a substantial mitigation.
  • Persian Gulf Shipping Volumes and War-Risk Insurance Premiums: Continuous monitoring of the Lloyd's Joint War Committee classifications and premium rates. A sustained reduction in Hormuz transits below 15 million barrels per day for crude and 1 million barrels per day for naphtha would trigger the severe impact scenarios.
  • Lake Gatun Water Levels (Panama Canal): Tracking daily draft allowances and transit slot auctions. A drop back toward the critical 78–79 foot water level threshold by Q3 2026 would confirm a secondary logistics chokepoint materialising on top of the primary supply shock.
  • Peruvian Anchoveta Biomass Assessments: Ongoing evaluations from IMARPE regarding the Q4 2026 secondary fishing season. Further TAC reductions beyond the currently implemented 40 per cent would confirm the cascading impact on global organic fertiliser and livestock feed supplies.
