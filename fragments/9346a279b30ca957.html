Half Of US Data Centers Scheduled To Start In 2026, Will Be Canceled Or Delayed

Author: Tyler Durden Published: April 13, 2026 Source: ZeroHedge

Just over two years ago, ZeroHedge introduced the "Power Up America" investment thesis, examining energy and logistical requirements necessary for US dominance in the AI race against China. The analysis positioned this energy-focused basket as the next critical trade opportunity.

However, this thesis has since underperformed relative to AI data center and technology indices. Investor confidence has wavered regarding whether America can scale adequately to meet massive AI computing demands, with potentially severe consequences for record capital expenditure budgets.

Current Status: Significant Delays Expected

According to Canaccord Genuity analyst George Gianarikas and research from Sightline Climate, "the American data center boom is hitting a formidable wall of logistical friction." The analysis reveals that approximately half of the nation's planned 16-gigawatt capacity faces potential cancellation or delay, with only 5 gigawatts currently under construction.

Obstacles include: - Local permitting challenges - Community resistance - Overextended global supply chains for transformers and helium

The Sightline Climate 2026 Data Center Outlook indicates that 30-50% of approximately 16 gigawatts of planned US capacity faces risk, despite $700 billion in expected hyperscaler spending.

Transformer Shortage: Critical Bottleneck

Transformer procurement represents perhaps the most severe constraint. Before 2020, high-power transformers typically required 24-30 months for delivery. Current demand has extended timelines to five years in some cases.

According to GE Vernova's electrification division leader, AI companies "want something typically in less than 18 months," creating an impossible timeline given current supply conditions.

Beyond 2026: Worsening Outlook

By 2027, the situation deteriorates further. Approximately 21.5 gigawatts were announced, yet merely 6.3 gigawatts are actually under construction. For 2028-2032 projects, visibility largely disappears: 37 gigawatts remains planned without firm completion dates, with only 4.5 gigawatts having begun work.

Funding Gap Remains Unresolved

JPMorgan analysis indicates that $5 trillion total investment is necessary to fund the AI expansion cycle. Even accounting for hyperscaler capital expenditures and debt financing, the US government must contribute over $1 trillion to close the funding gap.

Growing Sociopolitical Opposition

Public sentiment increasingly opposes AI integration. The Maine House approved a moratorium on large-scale data centers through 2027. Quinnipiac University polling shows Americans growing increasingly wary of AI's expansion into healthcare, education, and daily life.

Sam Altman's home experienced a firebomb attack, reflecting broader societal tensions regarding AI's economic and social implications.

The Central Problem: Manufacturing Deficiency

The fundamental issue stems from decades of outsourced manufacturing. Despite government reshoring initiatives over the past decade, minimal capacity expansion has materialized. This creates an ironic dependency: America needs Chinese electrical components to compete against China technologically.

Critical electrical equipment—transformers, switchgear, batteries—cannot be sourced domestically at required volumes, forcing reliance on imports despite tariff policies and national security concerns.

Power Generation Challenges

Trump administration promises regarding nuclear renaissance remain unfulfilled, with no new plants breaking ground. Small modular reactors represent theoretical solutions but remain years away from commercial deployment and scaling.

Many data center projects are pursuing independent power generation. Notable examples include New Era Energy & Digital's 7-gigawatt Lea County project, Homer City's 4.5-gigawatt Pennsylvania coal-to-gas conversion, and Crusoe's 1.8-gigawatt Wyoming natural gas and renewables facility. However, waiting for grid capacity expansion could require a decade.

Conclusion

Canaccord warns that "without a radical acceleration in domestic manufacturing and grid integration, the digital expansion of the late 2020s risks stalling into a series of unfulfilled promises." Goldman Sachs consensus indicates acute compute constraints affecting fabs, data center permitting, power availability, memory production, and labor markets.

Energy constraints are mounting alongside sociopolitical opposition, with resolution remaining uncertain.
