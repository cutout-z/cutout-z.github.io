The AI model gap is bigger than you think

By Lisan al Gaib (@scaling01) 12:39 AM · May 3, 2026 · 296.2K Views

Like all good articles, this one is a reaction to low-effort engagement bait. This was the winner this time:

The main issue is that public indices often measure headline benchmark performance, not general capability at comparable inference budgets. Those are not the same thing.

On the Artificial Analysis Index, open models appear to have just caught up with, or slightly surpassed, Opus 4.5 with reasoning enabled. Taken at face value, that suggests a lag of roughly 5 months.

But even that likely understates the real gap.

First: Token Usage Is Ignored

The index largely ignores token usage. Test-time scaling lets models trade much higher inference cost for better benchmark scores. In other words, a model can borrow some of the performance of future models by spending far more tokens today.

On the Artificial Analysis Index, leading open models are often using roughly 1.5x to 2x as many tokens as Opus 4.5 to achieve similar or slightly better scores. That makes the raw score comparison misleading. A model that matches another model while using twice the tokens is not equally capable in the economically relevant sense. Latency matters too. Users are willing to pay for faster responses, as shown by the recent "fast mode" offerings from Anthropic and OpenAI. A model that reaches the same score by spending many more tokens is not equivalent in practice.

Second: Benchmark Domain Weighting

Many public indices overweight domains where open labs are especially focused: coding, computer use, and agentic tasks. These are real and important capabilities, but they are not the whole capability frontier. If open labs optimize especially hard for these domains, aggregate indices can make the open/closed gap look smaller than it really is. (tau^2-Bench is getting benchmaxxed and should be removed)

So yes, it is reasonable to think open models may only be 4–5 months behind on coding-heavy tasks. But that should not be generalized to all model capabilities.

Third: Recent Benchmarks Can Inflate Apparent Progress

Evals like AA-Omniscience, GDPval, and CritPT were released late in 2025. Models trained before their release, such as Opus 4.5, are less likely to have direct exposure to them.

Newer open models like Kimi-K2.6, GLM-5.1, and DeepSeek-V4-Pro may have more knowledge of these benchmarks or benchmark-adjacent material. That does not necessarily mean they memorized the answers, but benchmark diffusion into training data is a real phenomenon. Once an eval becomes public and widely discussed, its structure, examples, solution style, and sometimes even direct answers can leak into future training corpora.

That means newer models can look artificially strong on recently released evals, especially when compared to older closed models that were trained before those evals existed.

Fourth: The Gap Is Compressed by Distillation

Closed labs spend enormous amounts of money on data curation, human labeling, preference data, evaluations, red-teaming, synthetic data generation, and deployment infrastructure. Open labs generally do not have access to the same internal data pipelines or human-feedback systems at comparable scale.

But once closed models are available through APIs or chat products, some of that investment leaks into the open ecosystem. Open labs can use frontier closed-model outputs to generate synthetic data, reasoning traces, preference pairs, coding solutions, agent trajectories, and instruction-following examples.

So part of open-model progress is not purely independent catch-up. Some of it is downstream of closed-lab investment. Distillation makes open models look closer to the frontier than they would be in a world where closed labs did not provide broad public access to their strongest systems.

In other words, the comparison is not simply "open labs versus closed labs." It is partly "open labs plus leaked capability from closed labs versus closed labs."

Fifth: Closed Labs Pay a Release Tax

Frontier closed models typically undergo much more extensive red-teaming, safety testing, policy evaluation, internal evaluation, product hardening, and deployment preparation before release. That can easily add one or two months between "the model is capable" and "the model is publicly available." Open-source models consistently perform poorly on safety evals.

So even if open models are only 4–5 months behind on coding, the effective capability gap may be closer to 5–7 months once release delays are accounted for.

Sixth: Out-of-Distribution Evaluations Show a Larger Gap

On benchmarks and indices like ECI, SimpleBench, LisanBench, and WeirdML, open models appear closer to Grok-4 or GPT-5 than to the current frontier. That points to a broader 8–9-month lag in general capabilities.

Seventh: Important Capability Axes Are Poorly Measured

Finally, there are important axes of model capability that current benchmarks measure poorly, or barely measure at all.

Some of these are axes we know matter but do not yet benchmark well. Coding is a good example: we try to measure it, but many coding evals are still too short-horizon. Results from METR, Epoch AI, and UK AISI-style cyber evaluations suggest that we often do not let models run long enough to reveal their full agentic capability.

In many cases, evaluations should probably use much larger token budgets: 100M to 1B tokens for long-horizon tasks, not just short benchmark runs. But those evals are extremely expensive, so they remain rare.

This is an underappreciated axis where closed models may retain a large advantage. Closed labs are more likely to have the infrastructure, internal tooling, scaffolding, and evaluation budgets needed to train and assess models on very long-horizon tasks.

And then there are axes we are not even aware of yet. Every generation of models reveals new capabilities and new failure modes that previous benchmarks did not capture. So it would be a mistake to assume that public benchmark indices fully describe the frontier.

Summary

The best reading is this:

Open models may be only 4–5 months behind on coding-heavy, benchmark-visible tasks. But once you adjust for token usage, benchmark weighting, eval freshness, distillation, release delays, and harder-to-measure general capabilities, the gap is likely much larger and closer to 8 months.

When you consider the whole package: general capabilities, reasoning efficiency/token usage, response latency, reliability, safety, long-horizon agency — it is not very plausible that open models will be on par with GPT-5.5 within 3–4 months. That is not a defensible position.
